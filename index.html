Why lighting matters
Ambient light biases colour: incandescent bulbs skew warm/orange, shade and overcast sky skew blue, mixed lighting skews unpredictably. A camera flash is close to neutral white light. For the most objective reading, turn off room lights, shoot the object in the dark with the flash on, then crop tightly to the object and avoid any area lit by outside light bleeding into frame.
Tips & tricks — common mistakes, read before shooting

Flash distance: avoid the hotspot

Holding the object too close to the flash sends most of the light straight back into the lens, which blows out a bright white glare spot in the middle of the frame. That spot isn't the object's real colour — it's overexposed reflection, and sampling it will give you a false near-white reading. Hold the object 20–40cm (about a forearm's length) from the flash so the light spreads evenly across the surface instead of bouncing straight back.

Too close
Flash reflects straight back — blown-out white hotspot
Good distance
Even light across the surface, true colour
Too far
Light falls off — image reads dark and noisy

Spotting a bad shot before you crop

Check the preview at full size first. A small pure-white or grey blob with sharp edges near the centre of the object is almost always flash glare, not the object's colour — back up a little and reshoot. If instead the object looks dim, grainy, or noticeably darker than expected, the flash was too far away or too weak — move closer or reshoot with the flash forced on.

Crop away the glare, not just the background

If a reshoot isn't an option, a small hotspot near the edge of the object can sometimes be cropped out. Use the square selection box to keep only the evenly-lit area, and avoid any patch that looks brighter or more washed out than the surrounding surface.

Glossy, glass & metal surfaces don't work well

This method reads reflected light, so it assumes the surface scatters light evenly in every direction (matte / diffuse). Glass, polished metal, glazed ceramic, and anything glossy or wet instead mirror the flash and the room around it in a small hard streak — so you're sampling a reflection of your flash or surroundings, not the material's actual colour. Where possible, test a matte area of the object instead, or lightly matte the surface first (a light dusting of cornstarch/talc, matte spray, or even breath-fogging glass) before shooting.

Glass / metal
Mirrors flash & surroundings — hard streak, not true colour
Matte surface
Scatters light evenly — reliable colour read

Watch for the object's own shadow

An object casting a shadow onto itself or nearby surfaces is completely normal and not a problem on its own — the flash is a single point of light, so some shadow is expected. What matters is whether that shadow falls across the exact spot you're about to sample. A cord, handle, lid, or any part that sticks up in front of the object can throw a shadow onto the surface behind it — if that shadow lands on your crop area, you'll get a false dark reading. Rotate the object, move the offending part out of the light path, or simply crop your square selection to a shadow-free patch.

Shoot straight-on, not at an angle

A flash lights a small cone directly in front of it. If the phone is tilted relative to the object — even slightly — the brightest, most evenly-lit patch shifts off to one side instead of landing in the centre of frame, and the far side falls into shadow or uneven light. Hold the phone square to the object's surface (roughly parallel to it, not angled up, down, or sideways) so the lit area is centred and even across the region you plan to crop.

Tilted
Hotspot drifts to one side, off-centre
Straight-on
Hotspot centred and even

Keep the object still

Many phone cameras don't take a single flash exposure — they quietly stack several frames together for noise reduction, or bracket exposures for HDR, and some of those extra frames are captured without the flash firing, under ambient light only. If the object or the phone moves even slightly during that burst, the stacked result can blend in a blurred or ambient-lit frame, quietly shifting the colour away from the true flash-lit reading. Brace the phone (or object) and hold everything still for a moment before and after the shutter fires, not just at the instant you tap it.

Stacked frames blur, may mix in an unlit frame
Held still
